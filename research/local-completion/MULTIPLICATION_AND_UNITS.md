# Multiplication and units in the local graph completion

30 September 2026. A follow-up to the
[representation theorem](REPRESENTATION_THEOREM.md), for the same Cartesian
product and the same seminorms. All statements below concern this specific
completion. No priority claim is made.

## Results

The real algebra $\mathcal A_{\mathrm{loc}}$ is an **integral domain**.
The proof also works over the complex numbers. In particular, completion
introduces no zero divisors, nonzero nilpotents, or nontrivial idempotents.

There is an exact recursive characterization of its units: the isolated-vertex
coefficient must be nonzero, and the uniquely determined formal reciprocal
at every radius must satisfy every polynomial weighted summability condition.
The representation theorem ensures that these reciprocals automatically
satisfy mass-transport balance.

The closed subalgebra generated by $H=K_2/2$ is the real-coefficient form of
$A^\infty(\overline{\mathbb D})$, with its usual topology. Its units, even when
invertibility is tested in the whole graph algebra, are precisely the
functions without zeros on the closed unit disk.

The full unit group is **not open**. There are explicit finite graph
combinations $X_N\to1$ with $X_N$ noninvertible and with degree generating
function identically $1$. In fact, nonunits are dense. Inversion is nevertheless
continuous on the unit group with its relative topology.

These results do not provide a finite decision procedure for arbitrary units,
a classification of all characters, or an analytic topology on the fraction
field.

The subsequent [character and inversion note](CHARACTERS_AND_INVERSION.md)
proves semisimplicity and sharpens the unit test: for an element already
in this completion, unweighted absolute summability of every formal
local reciprocal implies all its polynomial moments. The weighted
criterion below remains equivalent; its extra moment tests are automatic.

## 1. Local convolution and finite divisibility

Write $\mathcal B_r$ for the finite connected rooted simple graphs of root
eccentricity at most $r$, up to rooted isomorphism. Their product is

$$
B\star_r D=B_r(B\square D,(o_B,o_D)).
$$

It is associative and commutative, with unit $e=K_1$. Put

$$
L_r=\left\{a:\mathcal B_r\to\mathbb R:
\sum_B|B|^k|a(B)|<\infty\ \text{for every }k\ge1\right\}.
$$

Its multiplication is

$$
(a*b)(B)=\sum_{C\star_r D=B}a(C)b(D).
\tag{1}
$$

For $r\ge1$, let $\delta(B)$ be the degree of the root. Then

$$
\delta(C\star_r D)=\delta(C)+\delta(D),\qquad
\delta(B)=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ B=e.
\tag{2}
$$

Both factors embed along the coordinate axes in their product ball, and
those axes intersect only at the root. Hence

$$
|C\star_r D|\ge |C|+|D|-1.
\tag{3}
$$

Consequently a fixed ball has only finitely many pairs of factors: each
factor has at most its number of vertices, and there are finitely many rooted
simple graphs of bounded size. Formula (1) therefore makes sense even for
arbitrary formal coefficient arrays, without summability. Associativity also
holds there, since a fixed coefficient involves only finitely many triples
of factors. The weighted convolution algebras are subalgebras of this formal
algebra.

## 2. Separating additive invariants of rooted balls

The essential step is to replace separating rooted homomorphism counts by
additive invariants for $\star_r$.

### 2.1. An edge-coloring coalgebra

Use connected rooted loopless **multigraphs** $F$ as counting patterns.
Parallel edges are retained, with their occurrences distinguished when
colorings are counted. The target graphs remain simple.

Given an edge coloring $\sigma:E(F)\to[n]$, form $F_i^\sigma$ by contracting
all edges with color different from $i$ and retaining every edge of color $i$,
with its multiplicity. Inherit the root. If any retained edge becomes a loop,
declare the entire coloring invalid and its term zero. Each valid quotient
is connected. Its root eccentricity is at most that of $F$.

Define, on the rational vector space with these rooted multigraph classes
as basis,

$$
\Delta F=\sum_{\sigma:E(F)\to[2]\ \mathrm{valid}}
F_1^\sigma\otimes F_2^\sigma.
\tag{4}
$$

This definition is invariant under isomorphism. Swapping the colors proves
cocommutativity. Iterating in either order amounts to assigning one of three
colors to each original edge, and then contracting the other two colors
for each factor. The loop conventions agree: a loop created at an intermediate
stage remains a loop in a final retained factor; a final loop invalidates
the corresponding stage. Thus both iterates are the same sum over valid
three-colorings. This proves coassociativity; the same argument gives all
iterated coproducts.

The counit is $\varepsilon(e)=1$ and $\varepsilon(F)=0$ for $F\ne e$.
The coalgebra is connected and graded by the number of edge occurrences:
the degrees of the factors in every valid term add to $|E(F)|$.
In its reduced coproduct both factors have strictly smaller positive degree.

Fix $r$. Patterns of root eccentricity at most $r$ span a subcoalgebra.
For a rooted target $B$, let $h_F(B)$ count homomorphisms $F\to B$ taking root
to root. Parallel edges impose repeated copies of the same adjacency
condition. Write $\chi_B(F)=h_F(B)$.

If $F$ has root eccentricity at most $r$, every rooted homomorphism from $F$
to $B\square D$ lands in its $r$-ball. Each edge changes exactly one Cartesian
coordinate and so has a unique color. Its coordinate maps factor through
the two quotients above. Conversely, homomorphisms from a valid pair of
quotients determine exactly such a product homomorphism. Therefore

$$
h_F(B\star_r D)
=\sum_{\sigma:E(F)\to[2]\ \mathrm{valid}}
h_{F_1^\sigma}(B)h_{F_2^\sigma}(D).
\tag{5}
$$

Equivalently, $\chi_{B\star_r D}=\chi_B*\chi_D$, where this star denotes
convolution of linear functionals using (4).

### 2.2. Convolution logarithms

Since $\chi_B(e)=1$, its convolution logarithm is defined coefficientwise.
For a pattern with $m>0$ edge occurrences, set

$$
K_F(B)=
\sum_{n=1}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}
\sum_{\substack{\sigma:E(F)\twoheadrightarrow[n]\\
                 \sigma\ \mathrm{valid}}}
\prod_{i=1}^{n}h_{F_i^\sigma}(B),
\qquad K_e(B)=0.
\tag{6}
$$

This is precisely $\log_*(\chi_B)(F)$. Terms beyond $m$ vanish because
every nonunit factor uses at least one edge occurrence. The convolution
algebra is commutative, so the formal logarithm identity, applied in each
finite edge degree, gives

$$
\boxed{K_F(B\star_r D)=K_F(B)+K_F(D).}
\tag{7}
$$

The inverse exponential recovers all the $h_F$ from all the $K_F$.
Everything here is a finite polynomial identity on each pattern;
no convergence of an analytic logarithm is required.

These invariants separate $\mathcal B_r$. Indeed, equal $K_F$ values imply
equal rooted homomorphism counts for every allowed pattern. Partitioning
the vertices of a source by the fibers of a homomorphism, and applying
Möbius inversion on the vertex-partition lattice, recovers all rooted
injective homomorphism counts. A partition quotient is still connected
and has root eccentricity at most $r$; loops contribute zero, and parallel
edges cause no problem.

For two targets $B,D\in\mathcal B_r$ with equal counts, take the source to
be $B$ and then $D$. Since each has a root-preserving identity embedding,
there are rooted injective maps in both directions. Their vertex counts
are equal, then their edge counts are equal, and either map is a rooted
isomorphism. Thus $B=D$.

Each $K_F$ is local and has polynomial growth in the ball size: it is a
finite linear combination of products of finite homomorphism counts.

## 3. A multiplicative well-order and the domain theorem

Separation by additive invariants alone does not guarantee a well-order.
Rooted walk counts supply the required finite initial coordinates.

Let $W_j(B)$ count all length-$j$ walks starting at the root, with no
condition on the endpoint, and let $W_0=1$. For $j\le r$, such a walk
in the Cartesian product stays in its $r$-ball. Interleaving coordinate
steps gives

$$
W_j(B\star_r D)=\sum_{i=0}^{j}\binom ji W_i(B)W_{j-i}(D).
\tag{8}
$$

Define $\kappa_1,\ldots,\kappa_r$ through the formal identity

$$
\log\left(\sum_{j=0}^{r}W_j(B)\frac{t^j}{j!}\right)
=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\kappa_j(B)\frac{t^j}{j!}
\pmod {t^{r+1}}.
\tag{9}
$$

They are additive under $\star_r$. Explicitly,

$$
\kappa_n=W_n-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}
\binom{n-1}{j-1}\kappa_jW_{n-j}.
\tag{10}
$$

In particular they are integers, and $\kappa_n=W_n$ plus a polynomial
in the earlier walk counts.

Enumerate all connected rooted loopless multigraph patterns of root
eccentricity at most $r$. Order the balls lexicographically by

$$
\big(\kappa_1(B),\ldots,\kappa_r(B),
K_{F_1}(B),K_{F_2}(B),\ldots\big).
\tag{11}
$$

This is a total order by separation. It is a **well-order**. In any nonempty
set of balls first minimize $\kappa_1=W_1\ge0$. Once the preceding coordinates
are fixed, the preceding $W_j$ are fixed too, so the next $\kappa_n$ is
the nonnegative integer $W_n$ plus a fixed integer. It has a minimum.
After $r$ such choices the values $W_1,\ldots,W_r$ are fixed. Every vertex
can be reached by a walk of length at most $r$, so

$$
|B|\le1+\sum_{j=1}^{r}W_j(B).
\tag{12}
$$

Only finitely many rooted simple graphs remain, and their remaining
lexicographic coordinates have a least element.

All coordinates in (11) are additive. Consequently the order is strictly
preserved by multiplication:

$$
B<D\ \Longrightarrow\ B\star_r C<D\star_r C.
\tag{13}
$$

The unit is the least ball, since its root degree is zero and every other
ball has positive root degree.

**Local domain theorem.** The formal convolution algebra on $\mathcal B_r$
is a domain for every $r\ge1$.

Once the multiplicative well-order is established, this is the standard
generalized-series argument; see
[Imrich–Klep–Smertnig, Definition 3.4 and Propositions 3.7–3.8](https://math.smertnig.at/paper/graphproduct.pdf).
Those propositions apply directly over $\mathbb R$. The coefficient proof
below also applies over $\mathbb C$. The graph-specific input is the
well-order constructed above, not the general leading-term argument.

**Proof.** Let $a,b$ be nonzero formal arrays and let $A,B$ be the least
elements of their nonzero supports. In the coefficient of $A\star_r B$,
the pair $(A,B)$ is the only contributing pair. Every other support pair
has at least one larger factor and hence a strictly larger product by (13).
Thus

$$
(a*b)(A\star_r B)=a(A)b(B)\ne0.
\tag{14}
$$

In particular every $L_r$ is a domain. For $r=0$ the local algebra is just
the scalar field.

**Domain theorem for the completion.** $\mathcal A_{\mathrm{loc}}$ is an
integral domain.

**Proof.** If $X,Y\ne0$, the faithful array representation gives radii at
which their marginals are nonzero. By compatibility, every larger-radius
marginal of either element is also nonzero. Choose one common radius
$r\ge1$. The local domain theorem gives
$T_r(XY)=T_r(X)*T_r(Y)\ne0$, so $XY\ne0$.

The arguments work over any coefficient field of characteristic zero used
here, in particular $\mathbb R$ and $\mathbb C$. For the real completion
they give cancellation and the existence of an algebraic fraction field.
They do not supply a compatible completion or useful analytic topology
on that field.

## 4. An exact unit criterion

For $X\in\mathcal A_{\mathrm{loc}}$, write $a_r=T_r(X)$ and

$$
c=I(X)=a_r(e)\qquad(r\ge1).
$$

The equality across positive radii holds because truncation preserves
isolation. Evaluation at $e$ is multiplicative by (2).

If $c\ne0$, define a formal reciprocal $b_r$ by

$$
b_r(e)=c^{-1},\qquad
b_r(B)=-c^{-1}
\sum_{\substack{C\star_r D=B\\C\ne e}}
a_r(C)b_r(D)\quad(B\ne e).
\tag{15}
$$

Every sum is finite by (3). Every $D$ on the right has
$\delta(D)<\delta(B)$ by (2). This recursively defines every coefficient
and gives the unique formal inverse. Equivalently,

$$
b_r=c^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}
(\delta_e-c^{-1}a_r)^{*j}
\quad\text{coefficientwise}.
\tag{16}
$$

For coefficients of root degree at most $N$, only $j\le N$ can contribute.
No topological convergence is asserted in (16).

**Unit theorem.** The following are equivalent:

1. $X$ is a unit of $\mathcal A_{\mathrm{loc}}$.
2. $I(X)\ne0$ and the formal reciprocals (15) satisfy

$$
\boxed{\sum_{B\in\mathcal B_r}|B|^k|b_r(B)|<\infty
\quad\text{for every }r\ge1,\ k\ge1.}
\tag{17}
$$

When these hold, $T_r(X^{-1})=b_r$.

**Proof of necessity.** The isolated-vertex character takes units to nonzero
scalars. Each marginal of an inverse must be the unique formal reciprocal,
and membership supplies (17).

**Proof of sufficiency: compatibility.** Under (17), the $b_r$ are actual
inverses in the weighted local convolution algebras. Truncation is a
continuous unital algebra homomorphism, so truncating $b_s$ to a positive
radius $r$ gives an inverse of $a_r$. Formal uniqueness gives exactly
$b_r$. At radius zero, summing the inverse identity gives

$$
V(X)\sum_B b_1(B)=1.
\tag{18}
$$

Thus $V(X)\ne0$ follows from the proposed conditions; set $b_0=1/V(X)$.
The reciprocal arrays are compatible at every radius and have all the
required moments.

**Proof of sufficiency: balance.** Put $Z=1-X/c$ and

$$P_N=c^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{N}Z^j.$$

Each $P_N$ is an element of $\mathcal A_{\mathrm{loc}}$ and hence balanced.
Since $I(Z)=0$, its marginals agree with $b$ at every ball whose root degree
is at most $N$.

Given a local transport $F(G,u,v)$ of range $R$, truncate both endpoint
degrees:

$$
F_M(G,u,v)=F(G,u,v)
\mathbf1_{\{\deg(u)\le M\}}\mathbf1_{\{\deg(v)\le M\}}.
\tag{19}
$$

This is still local: an observation radius $R+1$ suffices. Its divergence
vanishes at every root of degree above $M$, for both its outgoing and its
incoming terms. Hence, for $N\ge M$, agreement of coefficients on those
root degrees gives

$$
\Lambda_b(\partial F_M)=\Lambda_{P_N}(\partial F_M)=0.
\tag{20}
$$

The equality is justified by absolute summability at, for example, radius
$2R+2$. If $|F|\le C|B_R|^k$, both the truncated and untruncated divergences
are bounded there by $2C|B_{2R+2}|^{k+1}$, independently of $M$.
They converge pointwise as $M\to\infty$. Dominated convergence against
the summable array $|b_{2R+2}|$ proves $\Lambda_b(\partial F)=0$.

The representation theorem now puts $b$ in $\mathcal A_{\mathrm{loc}}$.
Its local products with $X$ are the unit at every radius, so faithfulness
gives $Xb=1$.

This is a coefficient criterion, rather than an existence statement about
an unknown inverse or an approximating sequence. The outstanding analytic
test is the explicit infinite family (17). It does not reduce arbitrary
invertibility to finitely many calculations. Nor does it require a uniform
bound on total variation over all radii.

## 5. A retract onto a familiar analytic function algebra

Set $H=K_2/2$. Its $n$th power is the normalized counting element of the
$n$-dimensional hypercube. All roots have the same $r$-ball, of size

$$
b_r(n)=\sum_{j=0}^{\min(r,n)}\binom nj.
\tag{21}
$$

For $r\ge1$ these ball types are distinct for distinct $n$, since their
root degrees are $n$. Consequently, for every finite coefficient sequence,

$$
p_{r,k}\left(\sum_n c_nH^n\right)
=\sum_n|c_n|b_r(n)^k.
\tag{22}
$$

The bounds

$$b_1(n)=n+1,\qquad b_r(n)\le(n+1)^r\quad(r\ge1)$$

show that the induced topology is exactly that of

$$
\mathscr S_+=
\left\{(c_n)_{n\ge0}:
\sum_n|c_n|(n+1)^k<\infty\ \text{for every }k\ge1\right\}.
\tag{23}
$$

Multiplication is the Cauchy product. Its complex version is
$A^\infty(\overline{\mathbb D})$: functions holomorphic in the disk whose
derivatives of every order extend continuously to its closure. Indeed,
(23) gives uniform convergence of every differentiated Taylor series.
Conversely, smooth boundary values give Taylor coefficients decreasing
faster than every power by repeated integration by parts in the Fourier
coefficient formula. Two extra powers make every sum in (23) finite.
The same estimates identify the usual Fréchet topologies.
Over the reals take the subalgebra of functions with real Taylor coefficients.

This classical Fréchet algebra and its weighted Fourier-coefficient
description appear explicitly in
[Bhatt–Patel, *On Fréchet algebras of power series*, Example 1.5](https://repository.ias.ac.in/59672/1/9_PUB.pdf).
Here the graph-specific identification sends $H$ to the coordinate function.

For a general completed element define its degree generating function by

$$
D(X)(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}d_n(X)z^n,\qquad
d_n(X)=\sum_{\substack{B\in\mathcal B_1\\\deg(o_B)=n}}a_1(B).
\tag{24}
$$

Because $|B|=n+1$ at radius one,

$$
\sum_n|d_n(X)|(n+1)^k\le p_{1,k}(X).
\tag{25}
$$

Degree additivity proves that $D$ is a continuous unital algebra
homomorphism into the real form of $A^\infty(\overline{\mathbb D})$.
The continuous homomorphism

$$S(f)=\sum_{n\ge0}c_nH^n,\qquad f(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}c_nz^n$$

satisfies $D\circ S=\mathrm{id}$. Thus its image is a closed complemented
subalgebra, with continuous projection $S\circ D$, and is exactly the
closure of $\mathbb R[H]$.

The previously established observables are

$$I(X)=D(X)(0),\qquad V(X)=D(X)(1),\qquad
E(X)=\tfrac12D(X)'(1).$$

For each $|z|\le1$,

$$\Phi_z(X)=D(X)(z)$$

is a continuous complex-valued character. Evaluations outside the closed
disk are not asserted: polynomial moments do not ensure exponential moments.

**Units in this subalgebra.** $S(f)$ is invertible in the whole
$\mathcal A_{\mathrm{loc}}$ if and only if $f$ has no zeros on
$\overline{\mathbb D}$.

If $f(z)=0$ at any point of that disk, $\Phi_z$ obstructs an inverse.
If $f$ has no such zero, compactness bounds it away from zero. The reciprocal
$1/f$ is holomorphic inside and smooth up to the boundary, with real Taylor
coefficients when $f$ has them. Thus $S(1/f)$ is an inverse.

In particular,

$$
K_1+K_2\ \text{is not a unit},\qquad
I(K_1+K_2)=1,\quad V(K_1+K_2)=3,
$$

because $D(K_1+K_2)=1+2z$ vanishes at $z=-1/2$. Also, for real $t$,

$$
K_1-tK_2\ \text{is a unit}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ |2t|<1,
\qquad
(K_1-tK_2)^{-1}=\sum_{n\ge0}t^nK_2^n
\quad(|2t|<1).
\tag{26}
$$

Zero-freeness of $D(X)$ is necessary for a general unit. The next section
shows explicitly that it is not sufficient.

## 6. Nonunits converge to the identity

Let

$$m_j(G,o)=(A_G^j)_{oo},\qquad m_0=1$$

be the number of closed walks of length $j$ at the root. Define their
classical cumulants $q_j$ through the formal moment-generating series,
using the same recurrence (10), now with $m$ in place of $W$.

The Cartesian adjacency operator is the sum of the two commuting coordinate
adjacency operators. Therefore its rooted closed-walk moments satisfy the
binomial convolution (8), and

$$q_j(G\square G',(o,o'))=q_j(G,o)+q_j(G',o').$$

A closed walk of length $j$ cannot reach distance above $\lfloor j/2\rfloor$
from its root. Thus $q_j$ is determined by that induced ball, and the same
additivity holds for $\star_r$ whenever $j\le2r+1$.

Take any odd $N\ge3$ and $r=(N-1)/2$. The function

$$s_N(B)=\exp\!\left(\frac{\pi i}{2}q_N(B)\right)$$

is a bounded multiplicative function on $\mathcal B_r$, with $s_N(e)=1$.
Absolute convergence and convolution give a continuous character

$$
\chi_N(X)=\sum_{B\in\mathcal B_r}a_r(B)s_N(B),\qquad
|\chi_N(X)|\le\sum_B|a_r(B)|\le p_{r,1}(X).
\tag{27}
$$

Write $U(G)=G/|V(G)|$ for a nonempty finite graph, and define

$$
z_N=U(C_{2N})-U(C_N),\qquad X_N=1-\tfrac12z_N.
\tag{28}
$$

In $C_N$ all odd closed-walk moments below $N$ vanish, and $m_N=2$:
the two walks traverse the cycle once in opposite directions. In the
cumulant formula every other term contains a lower odd moment.
Hence $q_N(C_N,o)=2$. The even cycle $C_{2N}$ is bipartite, so every odd
moment and odd cumulant vanishes. Consequently

$$
\chi_N(U(C_N))=-1,\quad
\chi_N(U(C_{2N}))=1,\quad
\boxed{\chi_N(X_N)=0.}
\tag{29}
$$

Every $X_N$ is therefore a nonunit.

For a fixed observation radius $R$, if $N>2R+1$, both normalized cycles
have exactly the same $R$-ball histogram: the point mass at the path of
length $2R$ rooted at its midpoint. Thus

$$T_R(X_N)=T_R(1)\quad\text{eventually},\qquad X_N\longrightarrow1.$$

Both cycles are 2-regular, so there is also the exact identity

$$
D(X_N)\equiv1,\qquad I(X_N)=V(X_N)=1.
\tag{30}
$$

This supplies a noninvertibility obstruction undetected by every degree
character, even when all of those characters have value $1$.

**Consequences.** The unit group is not open. More strongly, nonunits are
dense: for every $X$, continuity gives $XX_N\to X$, while $XX_N$ is a
nonunit, since in a commutative unital algebra a unit product would make
both factors units. There is no neighborhood of $1$ on which the usual
unconditional Banach-algebra Neumann inversion argument applies.

**Relative continuity of inversion.** This does not prevent inversion from
being continuous on the unit group itself. Fix a unit $X$, a radius $r$,
and a weight $k$. In the weighted local Banach algebra, put
$a=T_rX$, $b=T_rX^{-1}$, and $\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{1,k}$. If $X'$ is a
unit and $\|T_rX'-a\|\|b\|<1$, the Banach-algebra estimate gives

$$
\|T_r(X'^{-1})-b\|
\le
\frac{\|b\|^2\|T_rX'-a\|}
{1-\|b\|\|T_rX'-a\|}.
\tag{31}
$$

Apply this separately to every defining seminorm. It proves continuity
of $X\mapsto X^{-1}$ on the relative unit group; it does not assert that
an arbitrary nearby $X'$ is a unit of the full completion.

## 7. Checks, dependencies, and bibliographic scope

The domain proof uses the original local convolution and faithful embedding.
The sufficiency direction of the unit criterion additionally uses the full
representation theorem. No unique factorization theorem for truncated
rooted balls is assumed.

The accompanying [verifier](verify_multiplication.py) records **21,697**
exact finite checks in [multiplication_results.json](multiplication_results.json).
It checks the coalgebra symbolically, including parallel-edge patterns,
against both iterated and direct three-color coproducts; compares the
homomorphism identity with direct root-preserving assignments; checks
logarithm/exponential reconstruction, cumulant additivity, and leading
coefficients; and checks reciprocal recursions and the hypercube and cycle
formulas. The small fixtures include all connected rooted types on at most
four vertices, at radii one through three. Odd-cycle examples run through
$N=13$. These calculations are regressions for the identities, not a proof
of the infinite statements or a machine-checked formalization.

The general use of cumulants and convolution logarithms has substantial
prior literature; see
[Ebrahimi-Fard and Patras, *Cumulants, free cumulants and half-shuffles*,
Sections 4, 7–8](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.5664).
The Cartesian product's connection to tensor independence and additive
adjacency operators is discussed by
[Accardi, Lenczewski and Sałapata, *Decompositions of the free product of
graphs*](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0609329).
These sources contextualize the cumulant methods. The ordered-series
argument and classical smooth disk algebra have the more direct sources
cited in Sections 3 and 5. The edge-coloring coalgebra, graph-specific
ordering, and reciprocal balance argument are given explicitly.
The [identification follow-up](FOLLOWUP_IDENTIFICATION.md) distinguishes
these exact standard constructions from the full balanced completion.
The [search log](SEARCH_LOG.md) records the coverage limits, and originality
remains unestablished.

The next structural questions include a description of all continuous
characters and maximal ideals, effective certificates for the sharpened
local inversion criterion, and what topology, if any, makes an
algebraic fraction field useful for the project's analytic aims.
